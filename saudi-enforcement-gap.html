The Saudi Enforcement Gap: When a Valid Contract Is Not Yet an Enforceable Asset

A deal can be legally valid and still be poorly designed for recovery. The gap appears when transaction documents, evidence, security and enforcement mechanics are treated as separate workstreams.

Jubran Alsughayyir6 September 20268 min read

Transaction lawyers usually draft for signing and closing. Credit teams think about default. Litigators arrive later. The strongest structure is the one that forces all three perspectives into the documents before money moves.

Saudi law makes that integration especially important because the path from contractual right to compulsory recovery depends on the legal character of the instrument, the evidence supporting the obligation, the perfection of any security and the procedural route available at default.

The best enforcement strategy is often drafted months or years before there is anything to enforce.

1. A contract is not automatically an execution-ready instrument

A valid agreement establishes rights and obligations. That does not necessarily mean the creditor can move directly to compulsory execution without first obtaining another instrument or decision recognised by the enforcement framework. Transaction teams should therefore identify at signing which payment obligations are expected to be immediately evidenced, which may require adjudication, and what records will be necessary if the obligation is disputed.

This is not merely procedural housekeeping. It changes how payment clauses, acknowledgments, notices, account statements, settlement mechanics and ancillary instruments should be drafted and retained.

2. Evidence architecture should be designed with the contract

Many commercial disputes are not lost because the contractual theory is weak. They become expensive because the factual record is fragmented. A contract can improve that record by specifying who certifies performance, how invoices are approved, when objections must be raised, which notices count, what electronic systems are authoritative and what happens if a party remains silent.

3. The new Enforcement Law raises the value of pre-default planning

Saudi Arabia published a new Enforcement Law in 2026. Its implementation framework reinforces a broader point: formal characteristics of enforcement instruments, registration requirements and execution mechanics cannot be left to the dispute stage. Deal teams should review the law and implementing measures applicable at the time of closing rather than relying on legacy assumptions.

The practical checklist should ask: Is any promissory or commercial paper being used? Does it require electronic registration or another formality? Is the person issuing it properly authorised? Are payment events consistent across the finance agreement and ancillary instruments? Are notices and acceleration mechanics aligned?

4. Security has value only if the creditor can reach it

Commercial documents sometimes speak of “security” as though it were a generic concept. In reality, each asset class can involve different creation, perfection, priority and enforcement mechanics. Shares, real estate, receivables, bank accounts and movable assets may require different formal steps.

The enforcement gap appears when the credit agreement says the debt is secured but the transaction file does not establish that every required local step was completed. Closing conditions should therefore distinguish between execution of the security document and completion of the acts that make the security effective against third parties.

5. Recovery is not the same as winning

A creditor can have a strong claim and still face a poor economic recovery if the debtor has insufficient assets, competing creditors or an insolvency process. The Bankruptcy Law makes insolvency analysis part of transaction design. Priority, security, avoidance risk and collective procedures can matter as much as the underlying contractual entitlement.

For lenders and investors, that means asking a harder question at inception: what assets are expected to exist if the transaction fails, who else may have claims against them, and what legal steps are required to preserve priority?

6. Draft the default scenario before signing

A useful transaction review should include a short “default simulation.” Assume the payment is not made. What notice goes out? Who signs it? What evidence shows the amount due? What cure period applies? What instrument is presented for enforcement? What security can be realised? What approvals or registrations must already have been completed? What happens if insolvency starts?

That exercise often exposes drafting inconsistencies that ordinary proofreading will never find.
