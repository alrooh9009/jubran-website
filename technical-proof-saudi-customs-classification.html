When the Lawyer Needs an Engineer: Technical Proof in Saudi Customs Classification Disputes

The tariff code is legal. The product is not. In many classification disputes, the decisive question is technical before it becomes legal.

Jubran Alsughayyir6 September 20268 min read

Customs classification disputes are often presented as arguments over competing tariff headings. That framing starts too late. Before choosing a heading, counsel must establish what the imported product actually is, how it functions, what components define it and which characteristics are objectively verifiable.

This creates an unusual evidentiary problem. Legal interpretation may determine how the Harmonized System is applied, but technical evidence determines the factual platform on which that interpretation operates.

A classification argument is only as strong as the technical description of the product it asks the tariff schedule to classify.

1. Classification begins with objective characteristics

The General Rules for the Interpretation of the Harmonized System direct classification through the wording of headings, relevant section or chapter notes and, where necessary, subsequent interpretive rules. In practice, that means product identity must be established with precision before legal comparison begins.

Descriptions such as “electrical device,” “control unit” or “industrial equipment” are usually too broad. Counsel should identify voltage, function, switching capacity, components, operating environment, installation method, whether the product is complete or part of another machine, and whether its principal function is independently performed.

2. Commercial descriptions can mislead

Invoices and purchase orders are useful evidence, but commercial naming conventions are not tariff law. A manufacturer may use a marketing label that groups several technically distinct products together. A distributor may describe an item by its project role rather than by the function relevant to the tariff headings.

That is why a classification file should not rely on the invoice description alone. Technical data sheets, manufacturer declarations, catalogues, diagrams, test reports and photographs may be more probative of the product’s objective characteristics.

Legal layerHeading text, chapter notes and interpretive rules.
Technical layerFunction, specifications, components and operating characteristics.
Evidentiary layerManufacturer documents, tests, certificates and inspection records.
Procedural layerObjection deadlines, record preservation and the issues actually raised before the authority.

3. The manufacturer can become a key witness without entering the courtroom

A carefully drafted manufacturer letter can resolve ambiguity that legal submissions alone cannot. The strongest letters do not merely announce a preferred HS code. They explain the product’s design, intended and actual function, measurable characteristics and distinction from products falling within the competing heading.

Where appropriate, the technical statement should be supported by datasheets, drawings or test documentation and authenticated in the form required for the dispute. A bare assertion that “the correct code is X” is much weaker than an engineering explanation showing why the characteristics fit X and not Y.

4. Inspection findings should be tested, not ignored

If customs has physically inspected the goods, counsel should identify precisely what the inspection established and what it did not. A visual inspection may confirm physical characteristics but not internal functionality. A product label may show voltage or model number but not the engineering function that determines classification.

The legal response should engage the inspection record directly: accept uncontested observations, challenge unsupported inferences and supply technical proof for characteristics the inspection could not determine.

5. Certificates are useful only for the proposition they prove

Conformity certificates, SABER documentation and certificates of origin can be important, but each serves a specific regulatory or evidentiary purpose. A conformity document may support product identity or standards compliance without independently deciding tariff classification.

The discipline is to ask of every exhibit: what exact proposition does this document prove? That keeps the record focused and prevents quantity of attachments from replacing evidentiary relevance.

6. Build the argument as a technical-legal chain

A strong submission can be organised in five steps: identify the competing tariff headings; state the legal classification rules; establish the relevant product characteristics through technical evidence; apply those characteristics to the heading text and notes; then explain why the competing classification requires a factual assumption contradicted by the record.

This method is more persuasive than beginning with the desired tariff number and working backward.

7. The broader lesson

Customs disputes demonstrate a wider principle in commercial law: some legal outcomes depend on expertise outside law. Lawyers handling energy equipment, software, construction defects, pharmaceutical products or complex machinery should know when the decisive factual issue belongs to an engineer, accountant or other specialist.

The lawyer’s role is not to imitate the expert. It is to convert expert evidence into the factual propositions the legal rule requires.
