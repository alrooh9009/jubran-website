True Sale or Hidden Security? Recharacterisation Risk in Saudi Securitisations

Selling receivables to an SPV can move credit risk and funding off the originator’s balance sheet. But the structure works only if the “sale” remains a sale when the originator fails.

Jubran Alsughayyir6 September 20269 min read

Securitisation depends on a deceptively simple proposition: assets are transferred from an originator to a special-purpose vehicle, and the SPV finances itself against the cash flows generated by those assets. The legal difficulty appears when the economic arrangements around the transfer begin to resemble secured lending more than a genuine disposal.

That distinction matters most in insolvency. If the transfer is respected as a true sale, the receivables should sit outside the originator’s estate subject to the applicable legal framework. If the arrangement is recharacterised as security, investors may find themselves exposed to the originator’s insolvency, priority rules and perfection defects.

A true sale is tested at the moment the originator can no longer perform. That is exactly when form alone is least persuasive.

1. What creates recharacterisation risk?

No single feature necessarily decides the analysis. Counsel should assess the transaction as a whole. Risk increases when the seller retains so much economic exposure or control that the transfer no longer behaves like an outright sale.

RecourseDoes the seller bear ordinary credit losses beyond limited representations or eligibility breaches?
RepurchaseAre repurchase obligations narrow and objective, or do they effectively guarantee performance?
ControlCan the SPV exercise meaningful rights over the receivables and collections?
PriceDoes the economics resemble purchase consideration or a loan advanced against collateral?

2. Limited recourse is not the same as no seller obligations

A workable securitisation still needs representations, warranties and remedies for ineligible assets, fraud, documentation defects or breaches by the originator. Those protections do not automatically undermine a sale. The drafting question is whether the seller is curing its own breach or absorbing the underlying debtor’s credit risk.

For example, a repurchase obligation triggered because a receivable never satisfied the eligibility criteria serves a different purpose from an obligation to repurchase simply because the customer later defaults.

3. Servicing can obscure ownership

Originators often remain servicer because they already have the systems and customer relationships needed to collect the receivables. Commercially, that is efficient. Legally, the servicing arrangement should make clear that collection activity does not reverse the asset transfer.

Key questions include: in what capacity does the servicer receive collections, how quickly are funds remitted, what happens to collections upon servicer default, and can the SPV replace the servicer? These questions lead directly to commingling risk.

4. Collections may be more important than receivables

An SPV can own a receivable while still losing economic value if cash collected on that receivable sits in the originator’s general account before being swept. If insolvency occurs during that interval, investors may face tracing, account-control or estate questions depending on the structure.

Well-designed transactions therefore focus not only on legal title to receivables but also on collection accounts, permitted withdrawals, sweep frequency, segregation, replacement mechanics and any account-control arrangements required by Saudi law.

5. Saudi regulatory rules already treat true sale as a legal opinion question

SAMA’s securitisation framework addresses true-sale requirements and requires legal analysis around the effectiveness of asset transfer in relevant regulated structures. That is significant because it recognises that accounting treatment and transaction labels are not enough: the transfer must work legally, including against insolvency risk.

Saudi capital-markets developments also make the question increasingly important as securitisation structures and investment products evolve. Transaction teams should distinguish clearly between regulatory eligibility, contractual transfer and insolvency effectiveness.

6. Bankruptcy analysis belongs at structuring stage

The Bankruptcy Law should be reviewed before finalising transfer mechanics, not after distress. Counsel should consider avoidance risks, timing of transfers, related-party features, perfection, competing claims and whether the SPV is sufficiently insulated from the originator’s operational and insolvency risks.

7. A practical true-sale checklist

  • Define precisely when title to each receivable transfers.
  • Separate seller-breach recourse from debtor-credit recourse.
  • Limit repurchase triggers to objectively justified circumstances.
  • Give the SPV meaningful control and replacement rights.
  • Design collections to minimise commingling.
  • Complete notices, registrations or other transfer formalities where applicable.
  • Test the structure under originator insolvency, not only under normal operations.
  • Obtain transaction-specific Saudi legal analysis rather than relying on labels such as “sale” or “assignment.”
