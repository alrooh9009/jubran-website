Financing Compute: The Legal Architecture of Saudi Arabia’s AI Data-Centre Boom

The legal challenge is not “AI regulation” in isolation. A hyperscale AI facility is simultaneously a power-intensive infrastructure project, a construction programme, a digital-services platform, a real-estate asset and a financing transaction.

Jubran Alsughayyir6 September 20269 min read

Saudi Arabia’s push into artificial-intelligence infrastructure creates a category of deal that does not fit comfortably inside one legal practice group. The asset may be called a data centre, but the commercial package can contain land rights, grid connection, power supply, EPC obligations, imported equipment, cloud-service regulation, cybersecurity, data governance, long-term capacity contracts and project or corporate finance.

The result is a coordination problem. A technology lawyer can understand the service layer while missing the bankability issue. A projects lawyer can understand EPC risk while missing data-transfer constraints. A finance lawyer can perfect security while overlooking whether the customer contract generates the stable cash flow on which the financing model depends.

In AI infrastructure, “compute” becomes bankable only when power, land, hardware, data, customers and lender rights are legally aligned.

1. Start with the physical bottleneck: power

AI workloads are unusually power-intensive. That makes energy availability more than an operational issue; it can be a condition to completion, capacity expansion and revenue generation. The legal package should test the certainty of connection rights, supply commitments, curtailment risk, commissioning milestones and responsibility for network upgrades.

If financing assumes a staged increase in megawatt capacity, the documents should reflect what happens when the power profile is delayed. Otherwise the project can reach physical completion while remaining commercially incomplete.

2. Land rights must match the financing horizon

Data centres require substantial fixed investment in buildings, cooling and electrical infrastructure. The tenure supporting that investment must be sufficiently durable and financeable. Counsel should examine ownership or lease rights, permitted use, development approvals, access, utilities, easements and the extent to which lender protections can attach to the project’s real-estate position.

3. EPC drafting must account for technology obsolescence

Ordinary construction risk is already complex. AI facilities add a second clock: technology changes faster than buildings. Specifications, change-control rights, approved vendors, testing standards and acceptance criteria should therefore distinguish between civil completion and technical readiness.

4. Customer contracts are part of the credit story

A financed data centre is only as bankable as the contractual quality of its revenues. Long-term capacity, hosting or cloud arrangements should be reviewed for minimum commitments, termination rights, service levels, credits, change-in-law allocation and credit support.

For lenders, a key question is whether the material customer contracts survive the precise events that create financial stress. If the project loses its anchor customer automatically upon a financing default or change of control, the security package may protect an asset whose revenue base has disappeared.

5. Data and cloud rules sit inside the transaction

Saudi digital infrastructure operates within a regulatory environment that includes cloud-computing requirements, cybersecurity expectations and personal-data rules. Counsel should identify which services are regulated, whether data localisation or transfer requirements apply, which party is controller or processor, and whether the project’s technical architecture is consistent with those obligations.

The Personal Data Protection Law and implementing framework make data governance a contractual issue, not simply an IT-policy issue. Allocation of responsibility for cross-border transfers, breaches, deletion, access and subcontractors should therefore be visible in the deal documents.

6. The security package should follow the operating model

Data-centre financings can involve security over shares, bank accounts, receivables, movable equipment, material contracts and real-estate rights. But the valuable asset may not be any single server or building. It may be the integrated operating platform.

This is where direct agreements matter. Lenders may need cure rights or step-in mechanisms with key counterparties such as landlords, power suppliers, EPC contractors and anchor customers. The purpose is not to run the business indefinitely; it is to prevent value from evaporating during a temporary default.

7. A Saudi “compute finance” checklist

  • Match land tenure to project and debt tenor.
  • Make power availability a defined legal milestone.
  • Separate construction completion from technical acceptance.
  • Map cloud, cybersecurity and data obligations to the operating model.
  • Test customer contracts for bankable revenue and survivability.
  • Align security with the assets and contracts that actually generate value.
  • Use direct agreements where termination risk could destroy lender value.
  • Model change-in-law and expansion rights for a rapidly evolving technology stack.
