The Red Flag Is Not the Risk: Turning Saudi Legal Due Diligence into Deal Terms

A diligence finding matters only when it changes a decision, a document or the economics of the transaction.

Jubran Alsughayyir6 September 20268 min read

Legal due diligence is often judged by completeness: how many documents were reviewed, how many issues were identified, how detailed the report became. For a buyer or investment committee, that is the wrong metric. The useful question is whether the legal team translated uncertainty into a transaction response.

A “red flag” is only an observation. The legal risk emerges from its probability, financial significance, regulatory consequences, timing and ability to be fixed before or after closing.

The strongest diligence report is not the one with the most red flags. It is the one that tells the deal team what each red flag should do to the deal.

1. Every material finding needs a destination

Once an issue is identified, counsel should decide where it belongs in the transaction architecture. Typical destinations include a condition precedent, specific indemnity, warranty, covenant, purchase-price adjustment, escrow, retention, closing deliverable or, in extreme cases, a recommendation not to proceed.

Fix before closingUse a condition precedent where the issue is remediable and closing should not occur until it is resolved.
Allocate historic riskUse a specific indemnity where a known pre-closing exposure cannot be eliminated.
Test factual accuracyUse warranties where the buyer needs a contractual statement of fact and a remedy if it proves false.
Manage post-closing conductUse covenants where the risk depends on future action or continued compliance.

2. Corporate irregularity is not one category of risk

A missing corporate approval, outdated commercial registration entry or inconsistency in constitutional documents can have very different consequences. The issue may be clerical and curable, or it may affect authority, ownership, dividend history, capital structure or validity of past acts.

The DD report should therefore avoid labels such as “corporate housekeeping issue” unless it explains the consequence and cure. If the issue affects the target’s authority to enter the sale or a material contract, it may belong in closing conditions. If it affects historic distributions, a specific indemnity may be more appropriate.

3. Licences should be analysed by dependency

A missing or expiring licence becomes material when revenue, assets or operations depend on it. Counsel should ask whether the licence is transferable, whether a change of control triggers consent, whether renewal is discretionary, and what happens to the business if the licence is suspended.

This turns a regulatory note into a deal question: must consent be obtained before closing, can the buyer accept a covenant to obtain it later, or is the licence so central that the transaction should not close without certainty?

4. Material contracts should be reviewed for transaction sensitivity

Contract review becomes valuable when it focuses on provisions activated by the deal: change of control, assignment, termination, exclusivity, minimum purchase obligations, customer concentration, unusual liability caps, security rights and consent requirements.

A contract generating a significant share of EBITDA deserves a different response from a low-value supplier agreement even if both contain the same change-of-control clause.

5. Litigation is a probability-and-recovery problem

A pending claim should not be reduced to “case exists.” Counsel should identify claim value, procedural stage, legal theory, available evidence, counterclaims, insurance, enforcement prospects and whether the target has adequately reserved for the exposure.

That assessment then informs price, indemnity, escrow or disclosure. The diligence team should not pretend to predict the court; it should make the uncertainty usable.

6. The report should speak two languages: law and transaction

A partner or investment committee should be able to read the executive section and immediately understand: what is wrong, how serious it is, whether it can be cured, who should bear it, and what document must change.

A practical red-flag format can therefore use five fields: finding, legal consequence, commercial consequence, recommended action and transaction-document location.

7. The final question: would this change the deal?

If the answer is no, the issue may belong in a lower-priority section rather than the red-flag summary. Materiality discipline is especially important in large Saudi corporate and private-equity transactions, where the volume of licences, employee records, property documents and contracts can otherwise overwhelm the decision-makers.
